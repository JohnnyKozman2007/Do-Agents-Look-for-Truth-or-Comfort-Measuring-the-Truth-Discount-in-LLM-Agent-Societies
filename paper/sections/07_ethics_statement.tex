\section*{Ethical Statement}
\label{sec:ethics}

This research investigates the mechanics of misinformation propagation, authority bias, and epistemic verification within synthetic multi-agent systems. We highlight both positive implications and potential risks associated with this work:

\paragraph{Societal Benefits and Defensive Applications.}
Understanding how autonomous LLM agents process and transmit unverified claims is an essential prerequisite for deploying reliable AI systems in multi-agent governance, distributed consensus, and civic deliberation. By demonstrating that social politeness and authority cues can suppress factual verification, our work informs the engineering of epistemic safeguards, automated fact-checking mechanisms, and robust auditing protocols to protect decentralized AI systems from cascading misinformation.

\paragraph{Misuse Considerations and Dual-Use Mitigation.}
A potential dual-use risk is that bad actors could exploit insights into agent authority bias and conversational compliance to engineer more persuasive automated disinformation bots or manipulate synthetic consensus in online forums. To mitigate this concern, our study intentionally focuses on innocuous, fictitious community concert announcements rather than weaponizable political, medical, or religious falsehoods. Furthermore, we do not provide generative prompt strategies for deceptive persuasion; rather, we provide open-source diagnostic tooling and metrics designed to identify and remediate epistemic vulnerabilities.

\paragraph{Human Subjects and Data Privacy.}
All experimental runs were conducted in synthetic environments using simulated personas and synthetic artist names. No human subjects were involved in the dialogue simulations, and no private personal identifiable information (PII) or copyrighted narrative materials were collected, stored, or processed.
